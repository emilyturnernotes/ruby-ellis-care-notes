Step 1: Check the Water Before Anything Else

Start by looking at the water in daylight. Cloudiness, a slimy feel on the steps, or a film along the waterline often points to chemistry that has drifted out of range. Color changes matter too. A light green tint usually means algae is starting, while a darker green or murky look needs attention soon.

If you have a basic test kit, check free chlorine, pH and total alkalinity once or twice a week. Write the numbers down. A simple log makes it much easier to spot a pattern, such as chlorine running low every hot afternoon, and it gives your service technician useful information.

Step 2: Skim, Brush and Empty the Baskets

Physical debris feeds problems. Leaves, pollen and insects break down in the water and use up sanitizer. Skim the surface with a net each time you walk past, and empty the skimmer and pump baskets before they overflow. A clogged basket makes the pump work harder and can reduce circulation.

Brush the walls, steps and waterline once a week. Stains and scale tend to cling to surfaces that get little movement, so brushing those areas keeps buildup from settling in. Vacuum the floor if debris has collected on the bottom.

Step 3: Balance Chemistry in the Right Order

Many homeowners add chemicals in the wrong order or all at once, then wonder why the water still looks wrong. Adjust total alkalinity first, then pH, and then sanitizer. Pour chemicals slowly, keep the pump running, and follow label directions for dosage. Never mix products in a bucket.

Heat and heavy rain both shift chemistry. Expect to test more often during a Texas summer, and after a big storm. If you find yourself correcting the water every few days, the cause may be something other than the chemicals, such as a filter that needs cleaning or equipment that is not running long enough.

Step 4: Watch the Pump and Filter

Pay attention to pressure gauges and sound. A pump that runs louder than usual, a filter that reads high pressure soon after cleaning, or water that does not return strongly from the jets can all signal trouble. Clean or backwash the filter according to its type, and note how often that needs to happen.

Stop using the pool until the water is tested and balanced. Brush the surfaces, run the filter longer, and call a professional if the color does not clear quickly. Persistent green water often needs a full recovery process rather than a single chemical dose.
